\section{Kalman Filter Experiment}
\label{sec:ch6.section2}
%----------------------------------------------------

A method is required to compare the output of the Kalman filter to known motion. This was achieved through the use of the Avia, a Stewart platform, and a motion capture system. To simulate the Avia recording data from a moving platform, a Stewart platform was used, and a motion capture system recorded ground-truth motion data for comparison and validation. A Stewart platform is a device that has the ability to manipulate its target platform in 6 degrees (linear translations on the X, Y and Z axes and rotation along the X, Y, and Z axes). The Stewart platform used was designed and built by Liam Clark, a former student for his Final Year Project. This platform has been in storage since 2018 having been used occasionally over the years, and has had many repairs and parts replaced. This has resulted in the platform performing irregularly and not up to the original design specifications. The use of the motion capture system overcomes this.
The motion capture system is manufactured by OptiTrack, who provided all sensors, software and supporting equipment. the system consists of 7 OptiTrack Prime 13 cameras, x of which are fitted with wide-angle lenses, and are all managed by a PC running OptiTrack's Motive software. The cameras are sensitive only to infrared light and tracking markers that reflect infrared light. The cameras are fixed in known locations around the target area and capture video of the target, while markers placed on the target allow the cameras to track the position of the target over time. Combining the different viewpoints of the cameras, the full motion of the target can be reconstructed.
As this experiment is only testing the attitude estimation capabilities of the Kalman filter, no translation of the platform will take place, only rotation. The Avia is attached to the Stewart Platform and rotated while recording IMU data for analysis. Motion capture markers are attached to the sensor to allow for the recording of ground-truth data.

The Avia was first rotated along single axes, first along the X-axis, and then along the Y-axis before being rotated on both the X and Y-axes, before finally being rotated along all axes (X, Y, and Z). The magnitude of rotation was set to 10° with frequency set to follow a chirp function, starting at a frequency of 0.005 Hz and ending at a frequency of 0.05 Hz. Each set of rotations occurred over 60 seconds. These amplitude and frequency parameters were obtained by inspecting IMU data from the SCALE cruise and selecting test values similar to those recorded during data collection.

\section{Normal Estimation Experiment}
\label{sec:ch6.section3}
%----------------------------------------------------

The following setup was devised to test the Avia's ability to resolve basic geometric shapes and their sizes.

%----------------------------------------------------
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{figs/field_boards.JPG}
    \caption{\textbf{boards}}
    \label{fig:boards}
\end{figure}
%----------------------------------------------------

Two sheets of hardboard, 1220 x 1220 mm each, were used to create a concave and convex surface. Holes were drilled in each corner of the board, and two pieces of string were used to deform the board and create a curved shape as shown in Figure \ref{fig:boards} above. The boards were placed on the rugby field at a distance of approximately 40 metres from the Avia, which was at a height of 10 metres (check these dimensions) above the field. The Avia was set up at an angle of depression of 16°. Two variations of the scene were collected, resulting in four variations of the target boards; concave and convex with the axis of curvature parallel to the sensor's X-axis (down the sensor line of sight) and with the board's axis of curvature parallel to the sensor's Y-axis (perpendicular to the sensor's line of sight).
